## Abstract

The three documents before this one each took a single broken symmetry of Einstein's physics and built its estimation problem: local position invariance and the gravitational coupling of the fine-structure constant; gravitational decoherence and the Diósi–Penrose length; the destruction of macroscopic superpositions and the momentum-diffusion coefficient. Each was a separate paper because each was a separate parameter. This document asks whether they are separate at all — and answers that, at the level that governs how well any of them can ever be measured, they are not.

The list of twenty proposed breakdowns of Einstein's symmetries — composition-dependent free fall, Lorentz violation at extreme energy, position-dependent constants, gravitational birefringence, anomalous heating, and the rest — looks like twenty unrelated experimental programmes. They share one structure, and the structure is exact, not rhetorical. **Each is a test of a symmetry, and a symmetry is generated.** Time-translation invariance is generated by the Hamiltonian. Space-translation invariance is generated by momentum. Rotational invariance is generated by angular momentum. Lorentz boost invariance is generated by the boost operator. Local position invariance is the statement that the generator of time-translation does not itself depend on position. A *violation* of any of these symmetries is, in every case, described by a coupling — a single number — that multiplies the symmetry's generator and introduces an evolution the symmetry forbids. To test the symmetry is to estimate that one coupling against zero.

And here the structure becomes a theorem. When a parameter θ is imprinted on a quantum probe through evolution by a generator G — the universal situation for a symmetry-violation coupling — the precision with which θ can be estimated is governed by the quantum Fisher information, and the quantum Fisher information is fixed by **the variance of G in the probe state**. For a pure probe the relation is an equality: the quantum Fisher information equals four times the variance of the generator. The attainable uncertainty on the coupling then obeys the quantum Cramér–Rao bound,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\;\ge\;\frac{1}{N\,\mathcal{F}_Q}\;=\;\frac{1}{4\,N\,\operatorname{Var}_\psi(G)},
$$

with N the number of independent probe interrogations. This is the single law this document asserts. **The detectability of any broken Einstein symmetry is bounded by the variance, in the probe, of that symmetry's generator.** Not by how long you integrate alone, not by how clever the apparatus is alone — by how widely the probe state is spread in the conjugate variable to the broken symmetry.

The TH(a,d) reading is, for the first time in this corpus, a reading of the *whole class* rather than one instance. The hidden sector — ker(F) — is the symmetry-violation coupling: one number per symmetry, zero if the symmetry holds. The observable sector — col(F) — is the probe state and what can be read from it. The boundary between them is the generator: the operator through which the kernel coupling acts on the column space. And the corpus's foundational inequality Var ≥ ℱ⁻¹ is here in its sharpest possible form — the quantum Cramér–Rao bound — with the Fisher information not assumed but *derived*, equal to the generator's variance. The generator is the conditional-independence boundary in the literal operator sense: it is the channel through which, and only through which, the hidden coupling reaches the observable probe.

This yields one genuinely new and genuinely useful statement, and the document claims only it. **To test a given Einstein symmetry better, you must prepare a probe with larger variance in that symmetry's generator** — and the twenty anomalies therefore sort not into twenty unrelated programmes but into a small number of *generator classes*, within each of which the same probe-design principle, and the same Heisenberg-limited entanglement enhancement, applies identically. A test of Lorentz violation and a test of any other boost-generated effect want the same kind of probe. The recent entanglement-enhanced symmetry tests — GHZ-state and spin-squeezed proposals reaching the Heisenberg limit — are not twenty separate tricks. They are one trick, applied per generator class, and the generator-variance bound is the reason it is one trick.

One law. A handful of generators. The twenty anomalies organized — honestly, by a real theorem — into the classes that share a probe.

## Part I · A Symmetry Is Generated, a Violation Is a Coupling

### I.1 A Thought Experiment: The Knob That Should Do Nothing

Every symmetry of physics can be stated as a knob that should do nothing.

Time-translation invariance: there is a knob that shifts every clock in your experiment forward by an interval. Turn it, and — if the symmetry holds — no measurable outcome changes. Space-translation invariance: a knob that slides the whole apparatus sideways; turning it changes nothing. Rotational invariance: a knob that rotates the laboratory; nothing changes. Lorentz boost invariance: a knob that sets the laboratory into uniform motion; nothing changes. Local position invariance: a knob that moves the apparatus deeper into a gravitational potential well; the *ratios* of all non-gravitational quantities are unchanged.

Each knob is a transformation, and each transformation, in quantum mechanics, is performed by a **generator**: an operator G such that turning the knob by an amount θ evolves the state by exp(−iGθ). The Hamiltonian generates the time knob. Momentum generates the space knob. Angular momentum generates the rotation knob. The boost operator generates the velocity knob. A symmetry is the statement that turning its knob produces no observable consequence — that the generator commutes with everything you can measure.

Now state a *violation* in the same language. A broken symmetry is a knob that was supposed to do nothing but does something — faintly. Turning it produces a tiny, real change in some outcome, and the size of that change per unit turn is a number. Call it θ, the symmetry-violation coupling. If θ = 0 the symmetry is exact. If θ ≠ 0 the symmetry is broken, and the magnitude of θ is the magnitude of the breaking. Every one of the twenty anomalies is, stripped to its mathematical core, a claim that some specific θ is not zero, and every experiment testing it is an apparatus for estimating that θ.

### I.2 The Twenty, Sorted by Generator

The organizing claim is concrete and checkable: the twenty proposed anomalies are tests of a *small number* of generators, and naming the generator of each is naming what kind of probe it needs.

- **Boost-generated.** Lorentz-invariance violation — GZK-limit breaches, Lorentz violation at extreme energy, gravitational-wave birefringence, the parity-violating splitting of light in strong gravity — these are violations of invariance under the velocity knob. Their generator is the boost operator. The coupling multiplies a boost-dependent term the symmetry forbids.
- **Time-translation / energy-generated.** The local-position-invariance redshift anomaly, the variation of constants, the differential ticking of distinct clocks in a potential well — these are violations of the statement that the time-translation generator, the Hamiltonian, is position-independent. Their generator is energy.
- **Space-translation / momentum-generated.** Non-commutative spacetime smearing, Planck-scale position jitter, anomalous phase noise in interferometers — violations of the space knob. Their generator is momentum.
- **Rotation / angular-momentum-generated.** Chirality-dependent gravitational coupling, gravitomagnetic spin–weight effects, geodetic spin-precession anomalies, anomalous gyroscope torques — violations of the rotation knob and of spin's coupling to geometry. Their generator is angular momentum.
- **Displacement / mass-coupling-generated.** The Eötvös anomaly, the Nordtvedt effect, chameleon-screened fifth forces, the Woodward mass fluctuation, gravitational Aharonov–Bohm phase — violations involving how mass or composition couples to a displacement or a potential. Their generator is a mass-weighted displacement.

This is not a claim that the twenty are *the same phenomenon* — they are emphatically not, and Part IV is explicit about that. It is the weaker, true claim that they are tests of a *small number of generators*, and that everything about how well each can be measured flows from which generator it belongs to.

### I.3 The col(F)/ker(F) Partition of a Symmetry Test

The TH(a,d) identification is exact and, for the first time in this corpus, applies to an entire class at once.

**ker(F) — the hidden sector — is the symmetry-violation coupling θ.** It is one number per broken symmetry. It is hidden in the precise sense that it is zero if the symmetry holds and carries no signature except through the generator. You cannot observe θ directly; you can only observe its action.

**col(F) — the observable sector — is the probe state and the measurements performed on it.** The probe is what carries Fisher information about θ; it is the column space, the estimable side.

**The boundary is the generator G.** This is the structural heart of the document. In every prior corpus framework the conditional-independence boundary was a surface, a horizon, an equation. Here it is an operator, and it is exactly the operator of the broken symmetry. The hidden coupling θ reaches the observable probe *only* through G — the evolution it induces is exp(−iGθ), and nothing about θ is visible except what G's action on the probe makes visible. The generator is the channel, the door, the boundary. What the probe can learn about the kernel is fixed by how the probe responds to the generator.

The rank–nullity statement is then the quantum Cramér–Rao bound itself, and Part II is its derivation.

## Part II · The Generator-Variance Bound

### II.1 A Thought Experiment: Why a Sharp Probe Cannot Feel a Faint Push

Imagine trying to detect a very faint, very slow rotation of a turntable. You place a marker on it and watch. If your marker is a single point dead at the center, it does not move no matter how the turntable turns — a point on the axis is insensitive to rotation. If your marker is instead spread far out toward the rim, the same faint rotation sweeps it through a visible arc. The *sensitivity to the rotation knob* depends entirely on how far the marker is spread out in the direction the knob acts.

This is the whole intuition, and it is exact. A symmetry-violation coupling θ acts on the probe through its generator G. How much the probe state *changes* when θ moves slightly — which is precisely what determines how well θ can be estimated — depends on how widely the probe is spread in the eigenvalues of G. A probe in a sharp eigenstate of G barely changes: turning the θ knob just multiplies it by a phase, an unobservable global phase, and the probe is *blind* to θ. A probe spread across many eigenvalues of G changes a great deal: the different eigencomponents acquire different phases, they interfere, and the interference pattern shifts measurably with θ.

The measure of "how widely spread in G" is the variance of G in the probe state. A probe with zero variance in G learns nothing about θ. A probe with large variance in G learns the most. The sensitivity is the spread.

### II.2 The Theorem

Make the intuition exact. A symmetry-violation coupling θ is imprinted on a probe by the evolution exp(−iGθ), with G the generator of the broken symmetry. The quantum Fisher information of the resulting state about θ — the quantity whose inverse bounds the estimation variance — is, for a pure probe state |ψ⟩, equal to four times the variance of the generator:

$$
\mathcal{F}_Q(\theta)\;=\;4\,\operatorname{Var}_\psi(G)\;=\;4\big(\langle\psi|G^2|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|G|\psi\rangle^2\big).
$$

The attainable uncertainty on the coupling then satisfies the quantum Cramér–Rao bound,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\;\ge\;\frac{1}{N\,\mathcal{F}_Q(\theta)}\;=\;\frac{1}{4\,N\,\operatorname{Var}_\psi(G)}.
$$

Three things about this expression carry the whole content of the document:

- **The reach is set by the generator's variance, not by the apparatus's cleverness.** Two experiments testing the same symmetry, with the same N, with wildly different hardware, have the same fundamental bound if their probes have the same Var(G). The bound is a property of the probe and the symmetry, nothing else.
- **The variance is a property the experimenter controls.** Var(G) is set by *state preparation*. To test a symmetry better you do not need a different law of physics; you need a probe prepared with larger spread in that symmetry's generator. This is the design principle, and it is the same principle for every member of a generator class.
- **It is the corpus's Var ≥ ℱ⁻¹, with ℱ no longer assumed.** Every prior framework wrote the Cramér–Rao inequality and supplied the Fisher information by hand or by analogy. Here the Fisher information is *derived* — it is the generator variance — and the inequality is the quantum Cramér–Rao bound, the sharpest and most fundamental form the relation takes.

### II.3 The Heisenberg Limit Is the Generator-Variance Bound, Read for Entangled Probes

The generator-variance bound explains, in one line, why entanglement helps — and why it helps *identically* across every generator class.

For a probe of N independent, unentangled particles, the generator of the whole probe is a sum of single-particle generators, and the variance of a sum of independent terms grows linearly with N. The bound then improves as 1/N — the standard quantum limit. But the generator's variance is *not* capped at the linear value. For an entangled probe — a GHZ state, in which the N particles are placed in a coherent superposition spanning the extreme eigenvalues of the collective generator — the variance of the collective generator grows as N². The bound then improves as 1/N² — the Heisenberg limit.

This is the entire content of entanglement-enhanced symmetry testing, and the generator-variance bound shows it is *one* content, not twenty. The recent entanglement-enhanced Lorentz-violation proposals — GHZ states of spinor atoms reaching Δκ ∝ 1/(F²N), spin-squeezed ensembles in tweezer arrays beating the standard quantum limit — are instances of a single statement: maximize Var(G) by entangling the probe across the generator's spectrum. Whichever symmetry is being tested, whichever generator it belongs to, the optimal probe is the GHZ-type state of that generator, and the enhancement is N² over the unentangled case. The twenty anomalies do not need twenty quantum-metrology breakthroughs. They need one — entanglement across the generator — applied per class.

## Part III · What the Bound Tells Each Programme to Do

### III.1 The Bound as a Design Instruction

The generator-variance bound is not a passive limit; it is an instruction, and the instruction is different for each generator class because the conjugate variable is different.

- **For energy-generated tests** — local position invariance, varying constants — the bound says: maximize the probe's variance in *energy*. A probe in a superposition of two atomic or nuclear states with a large energy gap has large Var(H); a probe in an energy eigenstate has none. This is exactly why a clock transition with a large frequency, or a nuclear transition with a large enhancement factor, is the right probe — and why two such transitions compared, spanning a large effective energy difference, sharpen the test. The previous document in this corpus on local position invariance found that the nuclear-clock enhancement factor lengthens the lever; the generator-variance bound is *why* — the enhancement factor is, structurally, an amplifier of the probe's energy variance.
- **For boost-generated tests** — Lorentz violation — the bound says: maximize the probe's variance in the *boost generator*. That means probes with large spread in momentum or in the boost-sensitive internal structure: high-energy particles, atomic states with large differential boost sensitivity, and entangled ensembles spanning those. It is why ultra-high-energy cosmic rays are powerful Lorentz-violation probes — extreme momentum is extreme boost-generator variance — and why entangled-ion Lorentz tests work by spanning boost-sensitive electronic states.
- **For momentum-generated tests** — spacetime smearing, Planck-scale jitter — the bound says: maximize variance in *momentum*, i.e. prepare probes spread widely in position-conjugate space, which means large, delocalized matter-wave states.
- **For angular-momentum-generated tests** — spin–geometry couplings, gyroscopic anomalies — the bound says: maximize variance in *angular momentum*: high-spin probes, large rotational superpositions, entangled spin ensembles.

In each case the bound converts a vague aspiration ("test the symmetry better") into a concrete, falsifiable specification of the probe. That conversion is the document's practical content.

### III.2 The Bound as a Triage

The generator-variance bound also tells an experimentalist, before any apparatus is built, *which* anomalies are reachable and which are not — by asking how large a generator variance is physically preparable.

A symmetry whose generator admits enormous probe variance — energy, for systems with large accessible level spacings; momentum, for large delocalized matter waves — is a symmetry whose violation can be pushed to extraordinarily small couplings. A symmetry whose generator admits only modest probe variance is one whose test saturates sooner. The bound is therefore a triage instrument: it ranks the twenty anomalies by attainable reach without a single experiment being performed, purely from the preparable variance of each generator. This is the kind of statement that belongs at the front of a research programme, and it falls directly out of the theorem.

### III.3 Why This Is the Corpus's Sharpest Identification

Every prior framework in this corpus identified a col(F)/ker(F) boundary in some system and asserted that the Cramér–Rao inequality governed it. The identification was, in every case, an interpretive overlay: the inequality was true but imported.

Here it is not imported. The symmetry-violation coupling *is* the kernel — one number, zero when the symmetry holds. The generator *is* the boundary — the operator through which alone the coupling acts. The quantum Fisher information *is* the generator variance — derived, not assumed, by the Braunstein–Caves identity. And the quantum Cramér–Rao bound *is* the rank–nullity statement of the partition — the precise, saturable relation between what the kernel hides and what the column space can estimate. For the class of symmetry-violation tests, the TH(a,d) partition is not an analogy laid over the physics. It is the physics: the structure of unitary parameter estimation, which is what a symmetry test mathematically is.

## Part IV · Scope, Honestly Stated

The reach of this document is bounded, and the bounds are the claim.

**It does not assert the twenty anomalies are one phenomenon.** They are twenty distinct hypotheses about twenty distinct couplings, and most of them are mutually exclusive — a universe with broken Lorentz invariance at the GZK scale is not the same universe as one with subluminal gravitons, and the two cannot both be the leading effect. This document asserts something far weaker and actually true: that the *estimation structure* of all twenty is identical, that each is a unitary encoding of a coupling through a symmetry generator, and that they therefore sort into a few generator classes sharing a probe-design principle. Organizing twenty programmes by their generator is not unifying twenty theories. It is a taxonomy of measurement, and it is offered only as that.

**The clean form of the theorem is the pure-state, unitary-encoding form.** The equality ℱ_Q = 4 Var(G) holds for pure probes under noiseless unitary encoding. Real probes decohere, and real symmetry violations may be encoded non-unitarily — the previous documents in this corpus on gravitational decoherence and on momentum diffusion are precisely about non-unitary, dissipative encodings, where the generator-variance equality becomes an inequality and the bound loosens. Those cases are not failures of this framework; they are its complement. The honest statement is that the generator-variance bound is the *ceiling* — the best any probe in a given generator class could do — and that decoherence is what separates a real experiment from that ceiling. A ceiling that real experiments fall short of is still the right thing to know first.

**The bound limits attainable precision; it does not predict a value of θ.** Whether any θ is non-zero is a question for the theories — string-theoretic Lorentz violation, scalar-field varying constants, modified gravity — that predict the couplings. This document supplies the estimation ceiling, not the prediction. It says how well each symmetry *can* be tested, given the right probe; it does not say what the test will find.

**It introduces no instrument.** The probes invoked — large-energy-gap clock superpositions, delocalized matter waves, high-spin ensembles, GHZ and spin-squeezed states — exist and are in use. The entanglement-enhanced symmetry-test proposals are in the literature. The only new object is the organizing theorem and its reading as the corpus's col(F)/ker(F) partition for the whole class.

The single defensible sentence is the one the abstract states and Part II proves: **a test of any broken Einstein symmetry is the estimation of one coupling, encoded on a probe through that symmetry's generator; the attainable variance on the coupling is bounded below by the inverse of four times the generator's variance in the probe; therefore the twenty anomalies sort into a few generator classes, within each of which the same probe — maximal generator variance, GHZ-entangled to the Heisenberg limit — is optimal.** It is a theorem, it is checkable, and it tells an experimentalist what to build. There is no machine with seven layers and no φ-suppressed prediction. There is one law, a handful of generators, and a design instruction.
